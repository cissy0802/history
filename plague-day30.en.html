Day 30 · How Plagues Shaped History

Germs Don't Decide: Four Plagues, Four Human Forks

Sunday, 26 July 2026 · BigCat's Time Machine
A plague looks like pure external force. But the final shape of every great epidemic is cut by human decisions: an order herding a country's population inside the walls, a statute pricing a ship's delay at thirty days, a parade held anyway. The pathogen supplies the conditions; people make the fork.
EVENT · 01

Everyone Inside the Walls: Pericles' Correct DecisionThe Plague of Athens · 430–426 BCE

430 BCEAthensThucydides, eyewitness
BACKGROUND & KEY FIGURES

The Peloponnesian War began in 431 BCE: Sparta's army was unbeatable on land, Athens' fleet unbeatable at sea. Pericles (c. 495–429 BCE) drew the militarily impeccable conclusion — abandon the Attic countryside, never give battle — moving the rural population inside the city and the Long Walls, supplied by sea until the enemy tired.

WHAT HAPPENED

Tens of thousands crowded in, housed in stifling improvised huts. In the summer of 430 the disease came ashore at Piraeus — the very supply line keeping the city alive. Thucydides caught it and survived, leaving in Book II of his history antiquity's coolest clinical record, and also its record of social dissolution: people stopped observing funerals and laws, because "judgment would arrive long after they were dead." Over four years Athens lost roughly a quarter to a third of its people. Pericles himself died of it in autumn 429; with the architect gone, Athens turned to the recklessness of Cleon and lost the war two decades later.

COUNTERFACTUAL + HISTORIANS' DEBATE

The counterfactual must sit on real constraints: without the evacuation, Attic farms would have burned year by year and Athens would probably have sued for peace sooner — he held no better card. The point is that he pushed the optimum on one dimension past the threshold on another: the same walls that stopped Sparta pushed population density past the epidemic threshold. The pathogen is still disputed (the reading of Salmonella Typhi from tooth pulp in the Kerameikos mass grave is heavily contested). More worth keeping is Donald Kagan's judgment: the plague did not decide the war — it removed the one man capable of restraining Athens' appetite for risk.

MODERN PARALLEL

Concentrating for efficiency — supply-chain hubs, a single cloud region — is the optimum in the normal state and an amplifier in the shocked one. Availability and survivability are not the same objective function.

ONE-LINE LESSON + QUESTION
A decision can be entirely correct on its own dimension and still be a catastrophe on another.
The metric you are optimizing — which variable you are not watching is it pushing toward a threshold?
EVENT · 02

Putting a Price on Plague: Ragusa Invents QuarantineRagusa Invents Quarantine · 1377

27 July 1377Ragusa (today Dubrovnik)thirty days
BACKGROUND & KEY FIGURES

The Black Death of 1347–51 did not depart; for three centuries it returned every decade or so. Ragusa was a city-state republic on the eastern Adriatic, fewer than ten thousand people, with almost all its income from entrepôt trade — stopping a merchant ship meant stopping its own bloodstream. Nobody knew the cause. There was only one empirical rule: a plague ship docks, and the city starts dying.

WHAT HAPPENED

On 27 July 1377 the Great Council of Ragusa passed the trentino: people and goods from infected regions had to spend thirty days outside the city, on the island of Mrkan or at Cavtat, before entry; violators shared the penalty. This is the first documented institutional quarantine — and what matters is not the idea of isolation but that it turned public health into an enforceable, priceable commercial rule: thirty days is a price. Too short and it leaks; too long and ships call elsewhere. Venice built the permanent Lazzaretto Vecchio in 1423 and later stretched the term to forty days — quaranta, hence quarantine.

COUNTERFACTUAL + HISTORIANS' DEBATE

Both counterfactual directions are hard. Without quarantine, Ragusa decays through repeated epidemics like most small Mediterranean ports. With a permanent embargo, the trade routes divert to Venice within a year. It had to find a number between two ways of dying. Historians disagree on where the number came from: an empirical fit to the incubation period, or a ready-made cultural template — forty days in the wilderness, Hippocrates' forty-day acute illness? Jane Stevens Crawshaw's work on the Venetian lazaretti (2012) leans to the latter: the institution went up on cultural intuition and was corrected afterwards by operating experience.

MODERN PARALLEL

Border rules and circuit-breaker thresholds in 2020 posed the same question: not "control or not," but where to set the number and who eats the cost. Every isolation policy is fundamentally an act of pricing.

ONE-LINE LESSON + QUESTION
An institution can work before the mechanism is understood — provided it prices the cost explicitly.
Which rule of yours runs on "can't say why, but it keeps working"? Who bears its cost?
EVENT · 03

Eighty Days: Smallpox and the Fall of TenochtitlanSmallpox and Tenochtitlan · 1520–1521

Sept 1520Valley of Mexicothe Exchange
BACKGROUND & KEY FIGURES

In June 1520, on the Noche Triste, Cortés was driven out of Tenochtitlan having lost half his force — fewer than a thousand Spaniards against a Mexica polity of some two hundred thousand. Months earlier, Narváez's expedition had brought from Cuba something the Americas had never held: smallpox. The indigenous population had no history of coexistence with it, and therefore no herd immunity.

WHAT HAPPENED

The epidemic reached the Valley of Mexico in September 1520. Cuitláhuac, the new tlatoani who had just beaten Cortés and was rebuilding the defense, reigned about eighty days before dying of smallpox. The Florentine Codex records that people "could not walk, they could only lie stretched out like corpses," with no one left to fetch water or cook — what the disease severed was not only lives but the chain of command. Cortés regrouped among the Tlaxcalans, laid siege in May 1521, and took the city on 13 August. The conquering force was never those few hundred Spaniards, but tens of thousands of anti-Mexica Tlaxcalan allies, plus a plague that hollowed out the opponent's political center.

COUNTERFACTUAL + HISTORIANS' DEBATE

Why did the germs flow one way only? Jared Diamond, Guns, Germs, and Steel (1997): Eurasia had domesticable herd mammals, and long human–animal proximity bred a reservoir of cross-species pathogens; the Americas had no such condition, so the exchange was asymmetric. Matthew Restall, Seven Myths of the Spanish Conquest (2003), answers back: crediting the outcome to germs erases indigenous political agency — the Tlaxcalans were not scenery; they placed a clear-eyed alliance bet, and merely failed to anticipate that the contract would not hold once the overlord changed. The population figures are themselves a battlefield: Cook and Borah put central Mexico at roughly a million by 1600, Rosenblat an order of magnitude lower.

MODERN PARALLEL

Globalization distributes risk unevenly by exposure history: the same shock is a head cold to the side with antibodies and a collapse to the side without.

ONE-LINE LESSON + QUESTION
Catastrophic outcomes rarely have a single cause; an external shock happens to land on an existing internal fracture.
Which internal fracture in your system is waiting for an external shock to turn it into a break?
EVENT · 04

The Parade Went AheadPhiladelphia, St. Louis, Harbin · 1918 / 1911

28 Sept 1918Philadelphiaa controlled experiment
BACKGROUND & KEY FIGURES

In the autumn of 1918 the United States was sprinting toward its fourth Liberty Loan, and under the Sedition Act newspapers printed no bad news (the name "Spanish flu" stuck only because neutral Spain did not censor). Philadelphia's health director Wilmer Krusen, knowing the navy yard already had hundreds of influenza cases, approved the Liberty Loan parade of 28 September — two hundred thousand people packed downtown. In the same weeks, St. Louis health commissioner Max Starkloff faced the same uncertainty and the same pressure from business.

WHAT HAPPENED

Seventy-two hours after the parade, every hospital bed in Philadelphia was full; more than 4,500 died in the first two weeks of October, with bodies left on stoops awaiting collection. Starkloff, two days after his first cases on 5 October, closed schools, theaters and churches and restricted gatherings, holding firm against commercial protest. St. Louis ended with roughly a third of Philadelphia's excess mortality — and the seventeen-city comparison by Hatchett and Markel (PNAS, 2007) confirmed the pattern: interventions that are early, fast and sustained track robustly with lower death peaks. Seven years earlier and half a world away, in the Manchurian pneumonic plague of 1910–11, Wu Lien-teh concluded the disease spread person to person by droplet rather than by rats as then believed, designed a double-layer gauze mask, and persuaded the court to cremate thousands of unburiable bodies at the end of January 1911 — a grave ethical offense at the time. The outbreak ended four months later.

COUNTERFACTUAL + HISTORIANS' DEBATE

Philadelphia's counterfactual needs no invention — St. Louis is the control: same country, same week, same virus, decision the only variable. But be fair about the constraints: Krusen faced a bond quota, a censored press and pressure not to weaken morale, so canceling carried a real cost then. Alfred Crosby, America's Forgotten Pandemic (1989), asks about the forgetting that followed: why did a pandemic that killed fifty million worldwide leave almost no trace in American public memory? Because it was swallowed by the war narrative, and because the deaths were scattered across ten million households with no single dramatic scene.

MODERN PARALLEL

The divergence between states in 2020 replayed Philadelphia and St. Louis: the difference was not knowledge but who was willing to bear the political cost of acting before the evidence was complete.

ONE-LINE LESSON + QUESTION
Against exponential growth the cost of a decision lies not in its direction but in its timing — three days late equals a third wrong.
Your last "let's wait for more data" call — how would the ledger read if it had come three days earlier?

Going Deeper

One — cross-case analogy: Ragusa's thirty days and St. Louis's two days were both "acting early without understanding the mechanism." Why did one become an institution and the other only a personal achievement?
The difference is whether the cost was written into the rule in plain figures. Ragusa wrote isolation as a statute applying uniformly to all shipping: the cost was predictable and calculable, merchants could schedule around it, and so it outlived any individual's tenure for three centuries. Starkloff's closures rested on him personally absorbing the pressure; when the pressure lifted they sprang back, leaving no clause to fire automatically next time. Institutionalization is not doing the right thing once — it is fixing how the cost gets shared.
Two — decision-theory mapping: why is "wait for more evidence" almost always wrong in an epidemic?
Because the time structure of gains and costs is asymmetric: the information gained by waiting is linear, while the cost accumulates exponentially. The fix is not sharper judgment but changing the shape of the decision — a pre-committed trigger threshold (close on the first local transmission), converting "judge" into "trigger." Automatic circuit breakers and stop-loss discipline are isomorphic: each concedes that real-time human judgment under pressure is unreliable, and outsources the decision to a rule in advance.
Three — complexity mapping: density brings both prosperity and fragility. Can the trade-off be escaped?
Cities, trade networks and data centers all derive value from connectivity, and the basic reproduction number rises with connectivity too — not a design flaw, but two faces of one quantity. What can be tuned is not density but the modularity of the network: high clustering with sparse cross-module links preserves local density gains while giving transmission a bottleneck. The quarantine station, the bulkhead, the clearing house — each inserts a closable boundary back into a highly connected network.
