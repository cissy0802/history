Day 40 · A History of Everyday Life

Everything You Do Without Thinking, Someone Once Decided

Thursday, 6 August 2026 · BigCat's Time Machine
Checking the clock, shutting the door, taking a bath, using a fork — not one of these evolved naturally. Each has a specific year, a specific promoter, and a rationale that made sense at the time. Nor does the arrow point forward: on one of them, Europe went backwards for three hundred years.
EVENT · 01

The Moment the Clock Moved From the Church to the Town HallThe Work-Bell of Amiens · 1355

1355–1370Amiens · ParisLe Goff · Thompson · Voth
Background & Key Figures

Medieval time was task-based: a stretch of time ended when the job did. Church bells marked the canonical hours, which stretched and shrank with sunrise and sunset — a summer "hour" was longer than a winter one. In 1355 the cloth masters of Amiens petitioned King John II for a bell in the marketplace to sound the start of work, the meal break, and the end of the day. Petitions of exactly this kind open Jacques Le Goff's famous 1960 essay "Merchant's Time and Church's Time in the Middle Ages."

What Happened

The crown granted it. Over the following century the textile towns of Flanders and northern France raised their own work-bells, owned by the town hall rather than the church. In 1370 Charles V ordered every clock in Paris set to the royal palace's mechanical clock, built by Henri de Vic: hours of equal length replaced the elastic canonical hours. Four centuries later Josiah Wedgwood pushed the logic to its conclusion at his Etruria pottery — clocking-in tickets, a dedicated timekeeper, fines for lateness. That is where E.P. Thompson drew most of the evidence for his 1967 essay "Time, Work-Discipline, and Industrial Capitalism."

Counterfactual & Historiographical Debate

What if timekeeping had stayed in church hands? Thompson's answer is that time-discipline is itself a product of capitalism. But Hans-Joachim Voth, in Time and Work in England 1750–1830 (2000), reconstructed working hours from court witnesses answering "what were you doing at the time?" — and arrived at a correction. Annual hours worked did surge, but the increase came mainly from the disappearance of "St Monday" (the Monday workers took for themselves) and of religious holidays, not from longer days. What was confiscated was not your hours; it was your whole days.

Present-Day Parallel

The green presence dot, Slack read receipts, hours-tracking software — the fight is unchanged: who has the right to measure your time. Remote work removed the commute, a natural boundary between days, and so the whole day is once again a unit available for capture.

One-Line Lesson & A Question
Whoever holds the clock defines what counts as working.
How much of your day is carved up by someone else's timer?
EVENT · 02

The Corridor Was Invented So Servants Would Stop Walking Through Your BedroomThe Corridor · Coleshill House, c. 1650

c. 1650–1700Berkshire · LondonPratt · Pepys · Ariès · Vickery
Background & Key Figures

The Tudor great house was built enfilade: rooms opening into rooms, so reaching the innermost meant passing through all the others. Masters and servants routinely ate and slept in the same hall. "Private," at the time, carried something closer to its root sense — deprived of public standing — and was no compliment.

What Happened

In the 1650s the architect Roger Pratt designed Coleshill House in Berkshire for his cousin, running a continuous corridor down the central axis and adding a back staircase. He wrote down the reason: so that servants could move about without entering the family's rooms. Almost simultaneously, Samuel Pepys began his diary on 1 January 1660, written in shorthand — a form addressed to nobody but himself — and stopped in 1669 as his eyesight failed. By the 1690s corridors, door locks and private studies were standard in English houses.

Counterfactual & Historiographical Debate

Ariès and Duby's A History of Private Life (1985–87) established the classic claim: privacy is a modern invention, arriving alongside the nuclear family and the interior life. Amanda Vickery's Behind Closed Doors (2009) answers with locks, keys and probate inventories: privacy was not invented but fought for, and distributed very unequally — an eighteenth-century Englishwoman often had privacy over one lockable box and nothing else; servants had close to none. Architecture gave the master privacy by pushing the servants into a windowless passage.

Present-Day Parallel

The arms race of open-plan offices and noise-cancelling headphones; remote work converting private space back into a site of production. The boundary of privacy is still set by circulation and property, not by preference.

One-Line Lesson & A Question
Privacy is first an arrangement of space and ownership, and only second a psychological need.
When were you last genuinely unreachable? What made that state possible?
EVENT · 03

Europe Learned to Bathe, Then Forgot for Three CenturiesThe Closing of the Stews · Southwark, 1546

1546London · Paris · KaifengVigarello · Chadwick
Background & Key Figures

Medieval European cities had public baths as a matter of course: Paris counted more than twenty étuves in 1292, and the stews on London's Southwark bank were public facilities regulated by guild and city. In Song-dynasty Kaifeng bathhouses were likewise an established trade — the Dongjing Meng Hua Lu lists them as ordinary commerce. Bathing was urban infrastructure, not a luxury.

What Happened

Syphilis reached Europe in the late fifteenth century, plague kept recurring, and medicine supplied an explanation: hot water and steam open the pores and let miasma in. Bathhouses were reclassified as sources of contagion. In 1546 Henry VIII ordered every stew in Southwark shut. For nearly three hundred years elite European cleanliness shifted to "dry washing": not washing the body, but changing white linen often — the whiteness of the collar was the measure of cleanliness. Georges Vigarello's Concepts of Cleanliness (1988) records the extreme case: Louis XIV used a bathtub a handful of times in his life while changing several shirts a day. The return trip waited for Edwin Chadwick's 1842 report on the sanitary condition of the labouring population and the Baths and Washhouses Act of 1846.

Counterfactual & Historiographical Debate

The "filthy Middle Ages" is a myth pointed the wrong way: the era that stopped bathing was the one after the Renaissance. The dispute is over cause. Vigarello credits the medical theory (skin reconceived from barrier to vulnerability); others stress deforestation and rising firewood prices, making hot water a hard cost. Either way the conclusion holds: one self-consistent but wrong theory plus one real epidemic was enough to erase a mature public utility for three centuries. Not a loss of capability — a reversal of judgement.

Present-Day Parallel

Pandemic-era surface-disinfection protocols came and went; decades after antibacterial products went mainstream, microbiome research began pointing to the costs of over-cleaning. Hygiene standards are a function of theory — change the theory and the standard turns around.

One-Line Lesson & A Question
A wrong but self-consistent theory, paired with a real panic, can reverse centuries of common sense.
Which of your hygiene or safety habits has never actually been tested for effect?
EVENT · 04

The Man Who Brought the Fork to England Was Nicknamed "Fork-Bearer"Coryat's Fork · 1608, and Erasmus, 1530

1004 · 1530 · 1608Venice · London · ChinaErasmus · Elias · Duerr
Background & Key Figures

In 1004 the Byzantine princess Maria Argyropoulina married into the Venetian ducal family and ate with a small golden fork. The cleric Peter Damian denounced it as decadence, and when she later died of plague the sources recorded it as divine retribution. For six centuries afterwards Europeans took meat from a shared dish with hands and knife. In 1530 Erasmus published De civilitate morum puerilium, specific to the point of instructing boys not to return a bitten piece of meat to the common platter and not to blow their nose on the tablecloth — more than thirty editions in thirty years.

What Happened

In 1608 the English traveller Thomas Coryat brought a fork back from Italy and used it in London, where friends mocked him as Furcifer — Latin for fork-bearer, and also for scoundrel. Forks did not become common in England until the eighteenth century. Over the same span China moved in the opposite direction: before the Tang, dining was individual, each person served at a separate low table (traces survive in the Dunhuang murals and in The Night Revels of Han Xizai); once high tables and chairs spread across the Tang–Song transition, communal dining from shared dishes became the norm. Europe moved from the shared platter toward individual utensils; China moved from individual settings toward the shared table — in the same centuries, in opposite directions.

Counterfactual & Historiographical Debate

Norbert Elias's The Civilizing Process (1939) threads these trivial rules onto a single line: once the state monopolises violence, the court converts external constraint into self-restraint, and manners become the skin that power grows on the body. Hans Peter Duerr spent five volumes — The Myth of the Civilizing Process (1988–2002) — rebutting it point by point: medieval people were not without shame, only ashamed of different things, and Elias mistook the silence of the sources for the absence of the behaviour. China's reverse path is the hardest counterexample to the single-line story: if manners must evolve toward individuation, why did they run backwards here?

Present-Day Parallel

Video-call etiquette wrote itself in two or three years: camera on or off, when to mute, whether you may eat. Nobody designed it, yet it hardened faster than any employee handbook.

One-Line Lesson & A Question
Manners are not a gauge of how civilized a society is; they are a projection of a particular power structure onto the body.
Which rule on your team that "everyone just follows" was never actually decided by anyone?

Going Deeper

1. Why do everyday habits almost always change before anyone notices what caused the change?
Because habits do not spread by persuasion; they spread by environment. Once the bell stands in the marketplace, you obey it whether or not you agree; once the corridor exists, circulation decides for you who runs into whom. Habits are carried by buildings and objects, and when those are installed nobody treats it as a decision. Changing a group's behaviour is far cheaper through the environment than through belief — which is also why the method is so easily abused.
2. The baths stayed shut for three centuries — so common sense can regress. Under what conditions?
Three things must coincide: a theory that explains the phenomenon, a real disaster supplying emotional force, and no means of measurement capable of refuting the theory. In 1546 all three were present — miasma theory was coherent, syphilis and plague were real, and germ theory was three hundred years away. The test transfers directly: when your field shows "the theory makes sense + something just went wrong + we can't measure it," be careful about what is currently being written into policy. It may not be knowledge.
3. China moved from individual settings to shared dining, Europe the reverse. Is that fatal to Elias?
Not fatal, but enough to demote his law to a case. Elias's mechanism (monopoly on violence → courtly competition → self-restraint) holds in Western Europe; China's turn fits a different thread — chairs and high tables changed bodily posture, and posture changed what communal eating could be. That suggests a plainer path: the objects change first, and the manners are retroactively promoted to morality. The value of cross-civilizational comparison is not falsifying a theory but exposing where it stops applying.
4. Voth found the loss of St Monday mattered more than longer days. How does that transfer?
It warns you that the wrong unit of measurement hides the real loss. Track average daily hours and the industrial revolution's seizure of time is nearly invisible; switch to annual days worked and the picture changes at once. The modern version: is what's being eroded the hours in your day, or whole undisturbed days? The first can be met with self-discipline; the second only with an institutional boundary.
