The History of the Family Is Written in Ledgers Kept to Manage People

Friday, 14 August 2026 · BigCat's Time Machine
A decree that made witnesses a condition of marriage, a stack of parish registers recounted three centuries later, a list that merely tallied heads, a secular birth register—all four were records built for administration, and each settled a different question: what counts as a marriage, how many children to have, whom you live with, and who still counts as a child.
EVENT · 01

It Took One Decree and Two Witnesses to Make Marriage a Public FactThe Tametsi Decree · 11 November 1563, Trent

11 November 1563Council of Trent, Session 24Bossy 1985
Background and Key Figures

The consent doctrine settled by Pope Alexander III in the later twelfth century set the bar for marriage extraordinarily low: two people saying "I take you" in the present tense were married on the spot, with no priest, no witnesses and no parental consent required. Such "clandestine marriages" were illegal but valid—and since marriage carried inheritance and legitimacy with it, the church courts spent centuries drowning in cases nobody could prove.

What Happened

On 11 November 1563, Session 24 of the Council of Trent passed the decree Tametsi: from then on a marriage had to be contracted before the parish priest and at least two witnesses, and entered in a register, or it was void. It was the first time canon law let a matter of form decide whether a marriage existed at all, and the opposition was loud—many theologians held that it stripped two people of the right to contract for themselves. The Protestant side took another route: Luther called marriage "a worldly thing" in 1530, and the German territories set up marriage courts instead. England, having broken with Rome, left the hole open until the Hardwicke Marriage Act of 1753, and in the meantime a trade in unlicensed weddings grew up around London's Fleet Prison.

Counterfactual and Historiographical Debate

Without Tametsi, inheritance would have rested for centuries on spoken promises no court could verify. John Bossy's Christianity in the West 1400–1700 (1985) reads it as a shift of power: marriage stops being the business of the family and the neighbours and becomes the business of the parish. Diarmaid MacCulloch's Reformation (2003) stresses that it narrowed women's position—a private promise had once been a usable claim, and afterwards a woman without a ceremony had nothing. The dispute is over who gains: does provability shelter the weaker party, or the party who controls the ceremony?

Present-Day Parallel

Legal recognition for cohabitation and common-law partnership turns on the identical question: when an unregistered relationship goes wrong, what can either side actually prove?

One-Line Lesson and a Question
Whether a relationship is protected usually depends on whether it left behind evidence anyone can check.
Among your most important commitments, which exist only in conversation? If the other side denied them, what would you produce?
EVENT · 02

The French Cut Their Birth Rate Before Contraception ExistedFrance's Early Fertility Decline · c. 1780–1900

from c. 1780FranceHenry 1958 · Coale 1986
Background and Key Figures

The standard demographic-transition story runs: industrialize first, urbanize, watch infant mortality fall, and only then does fertility follow. France scrambled that order entirely—it was the first country in Europe to decline, while still overwhelmingly agrarian and without any modern means of contraception.

What Happened

In 1958 Louis Henry and Étienne Gautier of France's national demographic institute published La population de Crulai, paroisse normande. Using family reconstitution—matching a parish's baptism, marriage and burial entries by name to rebuild household after household—they could count how many children each wife bore at each age. The result: marital fertility in France had already begun falling before 1789, and the fall came after the second or third child, an unmistakable pattern of stopping once a family was large enough. The only method was withdrawal, which parish clergy denounced in their reports as "those fatal secrets." The consequence was a reversal of national weight: around 1800 France had roughly 29 million people, the largest population in western Europe; by 1910 France stood near 39.5 million and Germany at 65 million.

Counterfactual and Historiographical Debate

Had France held English fertility levels, the manpower gap of 1870 would not have been so stark, and the Third Republic's anxiety about "depopulation"—with the family subsidies that followed from it—would never have taken hold. The cause is still argued. The popular explanation is that the Napoleonic Code's partible inheritance (1804) pushed peasants to limit heirs so the land would not fragment, but the decline predates the Code; the timing does not fit. The Princeton European Fertility Project (directed by Ansley Coale, 1963–1986) compared more than seven hundred administrative districts and found the timing only weakly related to economic and urbanization measures, yet closely aligned with language and cultural regions—it behaves more like an idea spreading than a function of income.

Present-Day Parallel

East Asian fertility has collapsed far faster than income models predicted, while cash incentives barely move it: if the decline travels as an idea, price levers were never going to pull it.

One-Line Lesson and a Question
Behaviour often changes ahead of technology and income, carried by the discovery that a different life is available.
The last real behavioural change in your organization—was it driven by incentives, or by one person doing it first where others could see?
EVENT · 03

"Industrialization Destroyed the Extended Family" Was Disproved by Household ListsLaslett, Hajnal and the Household Listings · 1965–1972

1965–1972Cambridge · EnglandLaslett 1972 · Hajnal 1965
Background and Key Figures

Into the 1960s the sociological consensus still held that pre-industrial society lived in large multigenerational households, and that factories and cities broke them into nuclear ones. The claim had never been tested against data, because nobody had counted.

What Happened

In 1964 Peter Laslett and E. A. Wrigley founded the Cambridge Group for the History of Population and Social Structure and began systematically working through parish "listings of inhabitants"—head-counts copied out house by house so parishes could tax and administer poor relief. The 1972 volume Household and Family in Past Time reported that between 1574 and 1821 the English mean household size held steady at about 4.75, and the great majority were nuclear. Three generations under one roof was never the norm. In 1965 John Hajnal drew what became the Hajnal line, from Trieste to St Petersburg: west of it women typically first married in their mid-twenties and between ten and twenty percent never married at all; east of it marriage came early and was nearly universal. The mechanism behind it was life-cycle service—young people spent years working in someone else's household before marrying, saving until they could set up their own. In western Europe marriage was not a rite of passage but a start-up cost you had to pay off first.

Counterfactual and Historiographical Debate

Late marriage gave western Europe a demographic valve: in bad years people postponed weddings and births fell the same year—Malthus's preventive check. Without it, only famine is left to do the adjusting after the fact. Lutz Berkner (1972) pushed back with Austrian data: a listing is a snapshot, stem families appear only at one phase of the household cycle, and cross-sections systematically undercount them. The Chinese comparison is more interesting still. James Lee and Wang Feng's One Quarter of Humanity (1999) shows that China combined near-universal and very early marriage with marital fertility lower than Europe's, regulated through late first births, long breastfeeding and adoption—the same restraint, achieved on one side by individuals delaying the wedding and on the other by families acting within it.

Present-Day Parallel

First-marriage age rising with housing costs is the old start-up-cost mechanism in modern dress; and designing elder-care policy around the belief that families always used to absorb their old rests on a premise the listings already falsified.

One-Line Lesson and a Question
Most common knowledge about "how it used to be" was never counted; count it and it usually collapses.
Which of your current decisions leans on a historical premise "everyone knows"? Has anyone ever put numbers to it?
EVENT · 04

To Protect Children Under Nine, You First Have to Know Who Is NineFactory Inspectors and Birth Registration · 1833–1874, Britain

1833–1874BritainCunningham 1995
Background and Key Figures

Britain had already legislated limits on child working hours in 1802 and 1819, left enforcement to local magistrates, and watched it come to nothing. Philippe Ariès's Centuries of Childhood (1960) argued that the Middle Ages had no concept of childhood—hugely influential, and repeatedly revised since.

What Happened

The Factory Act of 1833 did something no previous act had: it created four Crown factory inspectors with powers to enter mills at will, summon, and fine. The text barred children under nine from textile mills and limited those aged nine to thirteen to nine hours a day plus two hours of schooling. Enforcement then hit a wall—an inspector could not prove how old a child was. England had only parish baptism registers, which recorded no date of birth and omitted nonconformists entirely; the act fell back on having a surgeon certify age from appearance, and parents routinely lied. The Births and Deaths Registration Act of 1836 created secular registration, effective the following year, but it placed no duty on parents to report, coverage stayed patchy, and only the 1874 amendment's penalties finally closed the gap. Forty-one years separated "childhood is protected by law" on paper from a law that could be enforced.

Counterfactual and Historiographical Debate

Without birth registration, an age threshold stays declaratory, and the later age-grading of compulsory schooling and the age of criminal responsibility have nothing to stand on. Linda Pollock's Forgotten Children (1983) answered Ariès with more than five hundred diaries and autobiographies: parents had always loved and mourned their children, and Ariès mistook the silence of his sources for an absence of feeling. Hugh Cunningham (1995) splits the difference: what was invented was not parental love but the norm that childhood should be separated from labour, together with the age records that made it enforceable.

Present-Day Parallel

The minimum-age rules now being imposed on children's internet use replay the bind of 1833: the threshold comes first, proving age has no reliable answer, and so we are back to estimating from appearance—only now the estimating is done by an algorithm.

One-Line Lesson and a Question
A rule drawn around a status can never be more effective than your ability to verify that status.
Which rule you have written depends on a precondition nobody can actually check? Who is that rule stopping today?

Going Deeper

Why is common knowledge about the family so much worse than common knowledge about war?
Because families file no dispatches. A war is recorded, contested and reviewed by both sides from the first day; marriage, childbearing and who slept under which roof left traces only when someone was being taxed or given poor relief—the ledgers were not about families, they merely caught them in passing. The vacancy gets filled by memory, literature and nostalgia, and all three favour the more dramatic version. The corollary: any domain where nobody is responsible for keeping records deserves suspicion about its received wisdom.
For the weaker party, is provability a protection or a barrier?
It depends on who bears the cost of proof. After Tametsi, rite and witnesses let a woman assert a claim to marriage—and reduced to nothing any union that never completed the rite. Birth registration made child-labour law enforceable, and made unregistered children institutionally nonexistent. The pattern: when verification is supplied free by the institution it shelters the weak; when the individual has to pay for it, it screens them out. Asking who pays for verification predicts outcomes better than asking whether the rule is fair.
If fertility moves mainly by the spread of ideas, where can policy get purchase?
The French case suggests the topology of the diffusion network matters more than the strength of the incentive: districts sharing a river valley or a dialect fell almost in step, while neighbours across a cultural boundary did not move at all. This is the same class of dynamics as epidemiology and technology-adoption curves—outcomes are set by connection structure and where the early adopters sit, not by the size of the push. If so, the marginal return on cash is bound to be low; what can actually change is which kinds of life are visible.
