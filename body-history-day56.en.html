Day 56 · A History of the Body

The Body Is Society’s Most Honest Ledger — and Its Handiest Tool

Saturday, 22 August 2026 · BigCat's Time Machine
Not one of the four turning points in the history of the body happened in medicine: a conscription physical, the collapse of a textile trade, an insurer’s actuarial table, and a twenty-six-day building occupation.

Four Turning Points

1829
The body becomes an economic indicator: what makes men short is poverty, not climate
1895–1928
Footbinding ends: machine yarn pulled out its economic foundation
1928–1951
Mass morning drill: the same routine, three different purposes in thirty years
1977
Disability redefined: from “this person is defective” to “this building has no ramp”
EVENT · 01

A Ruler Built to Fill Regiments Ended Up Measuring France’s WealthVillermé, Conscript Heights & the Birth of Anthropometric History · France, 1829

1829Paris / 86 départementsVillermé 1829 · Fogel 2004 · Komlos
Background & Key Figures

Louis-René Villermé (1782–1863) had served as a surgeon in Napoleon’s army. Conscription left behind one hard threshold: men under 1.57 m were exempt. That rule, written purely to fill regiments, quietly accumulated a nationwide, year-by-year record of bodies. The prevailing explanation for regional differences in height was climate and “race.”

What Happened

In 1829 Villermé published his memoir on the stature of Frenchmen in the inaugural volume of the Annales d’hygiène publique. He ranked the 86 départements by tax revenue and set that against the share of conscripts exempted for being too short. The relationship was close to monotonic: the poorer the département, the more young men were judged “too small.” His conclusion was blunt to the point of offence — height is set neither by latitude nor by bloodline, but by poverty. For the first time the body became an economic indicator you could compare across places. Eleven years later he applied the same method to cotton-mill families, and helped produce France’s first child-labour law in 1841.

Over the same span US output per head doubled while birth-cohort height fell by roughly 4 cm — Komlos’s “Antebellum Puzzle.” Aggregate growth and bodily welfare can diverge for decades.
Counterfactual + Historiographical Debate

Without that conscription archive, the standard-of-living debate over early industrialisation would still be text against text: Engels says worse, the optimists say better, and nobody can produce a checkable quantity. Robert Fogel (The Escape from Hunger and Premature Death, 2004) built “technophysio evolution” on this evidence: nutrition raises stature and stamina, which raise output. The dispute is over what height actually measures — Fogel weights caloric intake, while researchers on the Dutch case (Hans de Beer) argue that income distribution and public health mattered more than income per head.

Modern Parallel

The World Bank and WHO now read development off the under-five stunting rate rather than GDP per capita, to see whether growth actually landed on people. The move generalises: validate a flattering aggregate against a downstream metric that lives in the user and cannot be dressed up.

One-Line Lesson + Question
Aggregates lie. Metrics that live inside people do not.
Which of your “growth” numbers would change shape immediately if you read it off a metric that lives in the user?
EVENT · 02

What Ended Footbinding Was Probably Not the Imperial EdictThe End of Footbinding · China, 1895–1930s

1895–1928Shanghai / villages of north & northwest ChinaDorothy Ko 2005 · Bossen & Gates 2014
Background & Key Figures

From the late Ming onward, bound feet marked Han women across every social class. The early Qing emperors Shunzhi and Kangxi both issued strict bans; both lapsed. In 1895 the Englishwoman Alicia Little founded the Natural Feet Society in Shanghai; reformers wrote footbinding into their theory of a “weakened race”; in 1902 the Empress Dowager Cixi issued an edict urging its abandonment; in 1928 the Interior Ministry banned it outright.

What Happened

The official story closes there: progressive discourse defeats a barbarous custom. But the anthropologists Laurel Bossen and Hill Gates, interviewing some 1,800 elderly women in villages across north China, the northwest and Sichuan in the 2000s, found a different curve. The distribution of footbinding tracked girls’ handwork almost exactly. Spinning, weaving and mat-plaiting are sedentary trades — they want a workforce fixed indoors. Footbinding disappeared first not where the campaigning was loudest, but where machine-spun yarn and imported cloth arrived first. Once handloom income collapsed, binding a daughter’s feet stopped being an investment and became pure cost. The foundation went, and only then did the custom fall.

Counterfactual + Historiographical Debate

Absent the shock of mechanised textiles, edicts and exhortation alone would likely have repeated the fate of the Qing bans: decreed, then ignored. Dorothy Ko’s Cinderella’s Sisters (2005) offers a second revision — footbinding was not simply male oppression; it was embedded in women’s own craft, respectability and marriage bargaining. And the anti-binding movement, while freeing feet, redefined the female body as national property: from “my foot” to “the race’s foot.” Critics reply that the economic account underrates how powerfully nationalist rhetoric mobilised the cities.

Modern Parallel

Standards of beauty change far less through moral advocacy than through the collapse of the accounts that sustain them. Recommendation algorithms are now working the other way: they raise the economic return on a particular body shape, and appearance anxiety and cosmetic demand rise with it. However loud the case for body diversity, it cannot outbid the price the distribution system sets.

One-Line Lesson + Question
Customs are not argued away. They fall when the ledger that sustains them stops adding up.
Whatever “bad custom” you want abolished — who profits from it, or is spared effort by it? Without touching that ledger, how far can advocacy get?
EVENT · 03

A Building Held for Twenty-Six Days, and Disability Walked Out of the Medical FileThe Section 504 Sit-in · San Francisco, April 1977

April 1977San FranciscoOliver 1990 · Shakespeare 2006
Background & Key Figures

For the first half of the twentieth century, disability was something medicine and eugenics handled. In Buck v. Bell (1927) the US Supreme Court upheld compulsory sterilisation 8–1, Justice Holmes writing that “three generations of imbeciles are enough”; some 60,000 Americans were sterilised thereafter. Section 504 of the 1973 Rehabilitation Act said one thing: institutions receiving federal funds may not discriminate against disabled people. But the implementing regulations sat unsigned for four years — two administrations were costing out what retrofitting buildings and transit would take.

What Happened

On 5 April 1977 protests opened in ten cities at once. In San Francisco one group walked into the Department of Health, Education and Welfare offices at 50 United Nations Plaza and stayed. Judy Heumann (1947–2023) and Kitty Cone held more than a hundred people inside for twenty-six days — the longest occupation of a federal building in US history. The government cut the phones and the hot water; the Black Panther Party delivered hot meals daily. On 28 April, Secretary Califano signed the regulations unweakened. The real turn was conceptual: the problem shifted from “this person is defective” to “this building has no ramp.” Move the responsibility, and a whole class of available actions appears.

Counterfactual + Historiographical Debate

An alternative draft did exist: concentrate disabled students in dedicated facilities — separate but equal, reissued. Had it been signed, accessibility would have grown into a set of special provisions rather than a universal standard, and the 1990 Americans with Disabilities Act would likely have lost its footing. The scholarly split sits here too: Mike Oliver’s The Politics of Disablement (1990) holds that disability is produced entirely by the environment; Tom Shakespeare (2006) counters that erasing the pain of impairment itself leaves the most severely affected without a voice.

Modern Parallel

Accessibility spillovers are the classic general dividend: ramps serve anyone pushing a pram, captions serve anyone on a train. Software a11y works the same way — constraints written for the extreme case force you to turn implicit assumptions into explicit interfaces, and everyone’s experience improves along the way.

One-Line Lesson + Question
Moving a problem off the person and onto the environment is not a rhetorical concession. It expands the space of solutions.
Rewrite “he just isn’t up to it” as “our process gave him no interface.” Which actions become available immediately?
EVENT · 04

Nationwide Morning Exercise Began as an Insurer’s Actuarial TableRadio Calisthenics: From an Actuary’s Table to a Nation’s Morning · 1925–1951

1925–1951New York / Tokyo / BeijingKuroda Isamu 1999 · Huang Chin-lin 2001 · Foucault 1975
Background & Key Figures

Around 1925 the Metropolitan Life Insurance Company paid to broadcast exercise routines on American radio. The motive was not lofty: policyholders who die early cost the company money, and pushing mortality back a few years is a gain you can book. The first mover behind mass calisthenics was not the state. It was an actuarial table.

What Happened

An official of Japan’s Postal Life Insurance Bureau saw the programme while on a study visit to the United States. On 1 November 1928, rajio taisō went on the air under the banner of commemorating the emperor’s enthronement — with the same plain reason underneath: the insurance bureau wanted lower payouts. It was then wired into national fitness surveys and wartime mobilisation. On 24 November 1951 the preparatory committee of the All-China Sports Federation released China’s first set of broadcast calisthenics, rolled out nationwide from 1 December; both Yang Lie, who proposed it, and Liu Yizhen, who choreographed it, had studied in Japan, while the institutional model came from the Soviet GTO system established in 1931. The same bending and stretching served three different purposes in thirty years: cut claims, mobilise for war, forge a new citizenry.

Counterfactual + Historiographical Debate

Without radio, collective drill could only travel through schools and the army, never reaching adults, housewives and farmers — the medium set how far discipline could reach. Foucault’s Discipline and Punish (1975) would read all of this as the mass production of docile bodies; Kuroda Isamu’s history of radio calisthenics (1999) and Huang Chin-lin’s History, Body, Nation (2001) supply the other half: the collective rhythm itself delivered belonging and pleasure, which is exactly why it outperformed an order — power alone cannot explain why people genuinely did it every day.

Modern Parallel

The actuarial logic is back unchanged: Discovery’s Vitality programme in South Africa (from 1997) trades exercise data for premium discounts, and health insurance is now linked to wearable step counts. Only the granularity differs — a whole city once tuned to one channel; now everyone wears a watch. Synchronised discipline has become individualised scoring, and a score is harder to refuse than a command.

One-Line Lesson + Question
A widely accepted health habit usually starts on somebody’s cost sheet; see who is paying and you can see where it will bend next.
Which of the bodily data now being recorded about you will end up in someone else’s pricing model? Can you say no?

Going Deeper

Why is the body the hardest historical record to fake?
Bone length, enamel growth-arrest lines, conscription exemption rates — all are by-products left behind by people pursuing other ends, and cannot be revised after the fact. This kind of unintentionally generated evidence is extremely valuable; conversely, any data produced specifically to demonstrate achievement should be treated as promotional material.
Across the four cases, what actually changed bodily norms: discourse or the ledger?
Footbinding and radio calisthenics both point to the ledger — collapsing handloom income and claims pressure were the forces behind the rhetoric. But the 504 sit-in ran the other way: concept first, cost allocation second, and it took an occupation to move at all. The safer formulation: the ledger decides whether change happens on its own; discourse decides what shape it settles into.
How was the category of the “normal body” manufactured?
In 1835 Quetelet proposed l’homme moyen, applying the normal distribution to human measurement; the precursor of BMI is his. The statistical “average” was quickly read as the normative “ought,” and eugenics and standardised medical screening grew out of that slide (Ian Hacking, 1990). Today’s health algorithms and model evaluation benchmarks are still replaying it: a descriptive median, once inside a decision process, becomes a normative threshold.
What will trigger the next rewriting of bodily norms?
On the pattern of these four cases, the trigger will not appear at a medical press conference but wherever cost structures shift: wearables turn continuous vital signs into a priceable asset, GLP-1 drugs rewrite “weight is a matter of willpower” into “a purchasable intervention,” and remote work dismantled presence as proof of diligence. The question worth watching is not what the technology can do, but who acquires the right to record your body, and who owns the record — the power in that 1829 measuring stick is being handed to a watch.
